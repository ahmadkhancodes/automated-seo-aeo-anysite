AI-assisted blog posts are safe for search in 2027 when each one is made for readers and not for rankings, and Google's rule is about volume without value, not about the tool. Follow five rules: edit by hand, add something original, name the author, disclose how the post was made where readers might wonder, and never fake freshness. Facts below were checked in November 2026.

This is a short checklist, not legal advice. Everything here comes from Google's own documentation, which we opened and quoted word for word. Where the documentation is silent, we say so. If you run a content program, pair this list with the [content quality audit](/services/content-audit) described later in the post.


| Habit | Safer | Riskier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Editing | A named person edits and approves every post | Posts go live straight from the model |
| Originality | Each post adds data, a case or a tested process | Each post rewrites the top results |
| Authorship | A real byline and reviewer | No author, or an invented one |
| Freshness | The date changes only when the content does | Dates bumped to look new |
| Volume | Publish what you can review and measure | Generate pages on every keyword you can find |

## 1. Put a human editor between the draft and the page

Google's guidance on people-first content defines it this way: ["People-first content means content that's created primarily for people, and not to manipulate search engine rankings."](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) The same page lists a warning sign worth taping to your monitor: "Are you using extensive automation to produce content on many topics?"

It also describes what unreviewed output looks like to its systems: using generative AI to produce large amounts of text without manual oversight or curation. A human editor is the simplest answer. Give the editor real authority to reject a draft, and track how many drafts they reject. A review step that approves everything is not a review step.

Keep the editor's checklist short. Is the answer correct? Is every number sourced? Does the post say something the first page of results does not? If the honest answer to the last question is no, do not publish it.

## 2. Add something original to every post

The spam policy names the behavior it targets: ["Scaled content abuse is when many pages are generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings"](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies). It adds that this applies "no matter how it's created", so a human-written content farm is just as exposed as an AI-written one.

Originality does not need a research lab. It can be a screenshot of your own process, a small test you ran, a customer question you answer for the first time in writing, or a decision table that saves the reader an hour. Write down the original element before you draft, in one sentence. If you cannot, the topic is not ready.

## 3. Show who wrote it

Google's guidance asks a direct question: "Is it self-evident to your visitors who authored your content?" It strongly encourages accurate bylines where readers expect them, and it warns against fabricated creator profiles such as made-up names or generated headshots.

Use real people, as on our [editor profile page](/about/jane-doe). If a post is reviewed by an expert, name the expert and say what they checked. If nobody on your team has the expertise for a topic, pick a different topic. The same page says that of the factors it lists, trust is most important, and a made-up author is a fast way to lose it.

## 4. Say how the post was made when readers might wonder

The guidance continues: "Is the use of automation, including AI-generation, self-evident to visitors through disclosures or in other ways?" It is framed as a question to ask yourself, not as a mandatory label for every page.

A one-line note near the byline is enough for most sites: who wrote the post, who reviewed it, and whether AI tools helped with drafting or research. Keep it plain and factual. Place it where a curious reader will look, and use the same wording on every post so it does not read as an apology.

## 5. Never fake freshness or stuff keywords

Two habits look harmless and are named in the documentation. The first is the date. Google's helpful-content page warns against "changing the date of pages to make them seem fresh when the content has not substantially changed." If you refresh a post, change the facts and sources first, then the date, and note what changed.

The second is keyword stuffing, which the spam policy describes as "filling a web page with keywords or numbers in an attempt to manipulate rankings." In 2027 editions this matters twice over: a year in the headline is useful, the same year in every sentence is not. Mention it where a reader would expect it and stop.

## What changes in 2027 for AI-assisted publishers?

Three things are worth planning for, and we label our own reasoning as such.

First, the volume question gets harder, not easier. Models make it cheap to produce pages, which is exactly the situation the scaled-content policy describes. Our view: the sites that keep winning will be the ones that publish fewer pages and review each one harder.

Second, formats that once earned special treatment can disappear. Google's FAQ structured data page now carries a notice that FAQ rich results are [no longer shown in Google Search](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/faqpage). Write FAQ sections because readers and answer engines use them, not because of a snippet.

Third, assistants that summarize the web reward pages that state facts plainly and name their sources. That is an observation about how these tools are built, not a measured result, so treat it as a hypothesis and test it on your own pages.

## What would a 30-day pilot deliver?

A pilot is a small, measured run before you scale. A typical 30-day plan from Example Co, a fictional publisher used for this demonstration, looks like this:

- **Week 1:** you share your last ten posts and your editing process. We score each post against the five rules above.
- **Weeks 2 and 3:** you publish up to six posts under the checklist, each with a named editor, an original element and a disclosure line.
- **Week 4:** we compare those posts with the older ones on impressions, clicks and time on page, and decide what to scale.

Success is measured by the share of posts that pass review on the first try and by search impressions after the first month, not by how many pages went live. See our [editorial review service](/services/editorial-review) for the full scope.
